Method

What the detector reads, and an alert rule chosen without looking at the answer

The model here is ordinary — a scikit-learn Isolation Forest, which is the most common choice made on this dataset. What the project rests on is not the model but the layer after it: a lead-time metric built so that it cannot be tuned into a flattering number.

The feature scope, stated up front. The features below fall into two groups: those the published pipeline actually scores, and the defect-frequency machinery that is implemented and tested but not yet joined to it. The second group needs raw waveforms, and the pipeline scores a table of per-file summary statistics, so nothing in it reaches the model. The split is marked throughout, because the difference between what this project measures and what it is set up to measure is exactly the kind of thing a method page exists to be honest about.

Why a bearing defect has a frequency

A rolling-element bearing is a machine with known geometry, so a defect in it does not produce generic “vibration”. Each rolling element strikes the defect once per pass, and the pass rate follows from the geometry and shaft speed alone. Four rates matter, one per defect location:

Frequency Struck by Indicates
BPFO — ball pass frequency, outer race each ball passing a fixed outer-race point outer-race spall
BPFI — ball pass frequency, inner race each ball passing a point on the rotating inner race inner-race spall
BSF — ball spin frequency a defect on a ball itself, once per rotation rolling-element defect
FTF — fundamental train frequency the cage rotating cage damage

The data documentation carries the numbers: rig geometry, the four frequencies it yields for this bearing, their provenance, and which one carries the signal in which test. They are properties of the rig rather than choices made here, so they live with the data and are stated once.

This is what would make a fault locatable rather than merely detectable. Energy rising at the outer-race pass frequency and its harmonics says outer race — it does not say “something is wrong”. That is what the frequency-domain features are built to see, and it is the part not yet wired into the published result.

What the detector reads

loading.py reduces each 20,480-sample acquisition to six statistics per channel: RMS, mean, standard deviation, absolute maximum, excess kurtosis and skewness. features.py then adds, for the RMS and kurtosis columns only, rolling mean, standard deviation and maximum over windows of 10 and 50 files, plus first differences and their absolute values.

The detector selects from that table by substring — _rms, _kurt, _std, _max — and by the failed bearing’s prefix. Mean and skewness are computed and then not selected. For Test 3, Bearing 3 that is 20 columns; for Test 1, which carries two accelerometers per bearing, 40.

  • RMS — total vibration energy. The primary degradation indicator, and on this data it rises 11.4× above baseline before Test 3’s bearing fails.
  • Kurtosis — the fourth moment, sensitive to impulsiveness rather than amplitude. A spall produces sharp repeated impacts, which raise kurtosis while RMS is still near baseline, so it can lead RMS. Note this project computes excess kurtosis, where Gaussian reads 0 and not 3 — every threshold in the bearing literature is quoted 3 too high for this column. See the data documentation.
  • Standard deviation and absolute maximum — for a near-zero-mean signal the first is numerically close to RMS, and the second is the peak that a crest factor would be built from.
  • The rolling and difference columns are 16 of the 20. They carry the rate of degradation rather than its level, which is what lets an alert fire while the level itself is still unremarkable.

Every one of these is a time-domain quantity. None of them knows what a bearing is.

Implemented, and not yet joined to the pipeline

FeatureExtractor computes the following from a raw 1-D waveform. Each is exercised by tests/test_features.py, and notebook 02 demonstrates them on synthetic healthy and faulty signals — but the detection pipeline never loads a waveform, so none of them appears in the feature table the model sees.

Frequency domain, from a Welch power spectral density (PSD) estimate:

  • Defect-frequency band energy — power in narrow bands around BPFO, BPFI, BSF and FTF and their first three harmonics. This is the physics-informed feature proper: it asks the specific question the geometry poses.
  • Spectral entropy — how evenly energy is spread across the spectrum.
  • High-frequency ratio — proportion of energy above 5 kHz.

Time domain, shape ratios the summary table does not carry:

  • Crest factor and clearance factor — peak-to-average ratios, the same impulsiveness idea as kurtosis, normalised differently.

Joining these to the pipeline costs one pass over the 6.2 GB raw archive. Whether they earn their place over RMS and kurtosis alone is then an ablation rather than an assertion, and that experiment has not been run.

Three feature directions that are the opposite of the intuition

These are properties of the features in the section above — established on synthetic signals and by the test suite, not measured on this dataset’s published result, since none of them is in it. Each is a trap that produces a confidently wrong reading, so they are worth stating plainly, together with how firmly each is actually established, which differs between them.

  • Spectral entropy is higher in healthy bearings, not lower. The intuition says a fault means disorder means high entropy. The opposite holds: healthy bearing noise is broadband and close to flat, which is maximum entropy. A developing defect concentrates energy into BPFO harmonics — structure, not noise — and structure lowers entropy. This one is pinned by a test (test_spectral_entropy_faulty_lower_than_healthy, asserting healthy > faulty on synthetic signals), so it cannot silently regress.
  • high_freq_ratio runs the other way too, for the same underlying reason: healthy Gaussian noise is already rich in high frequencies, so there is little headroom for a fault to add any. Only the [0, 1] range is asserted by a test; the direction is documented but not pinned.
  • crest_factor becomes unreliable at high fault severity. The standard explanation is that it rises early, as discrete impacts appear against a quiet floor, then falls back once the defect is severe enough that impacts merge into a noise floor the fault itself has lifted — so the peak stops standing out from the average. Read monotonically, it will eventually suggest the bearing is recovering. Only crest_factor >= 1 is asserted by a test; the non-monotonicity is a documented caution here, not a measurement this project makes.

The uneven footing is deliberate to record rather than paper over: one direction is enforced by the suite, two are conventions carried from the literature and the feature definitions. Anything relying on the latter two should re-derive them first.

The detector

StandardScaler → PCA (10 components) → IsolationForest, fitted on the first 60% of each series on the assumption that a run-to-failure rig starts healthy, then scoring the whole series. Isolation Forest suits the problem because it needs no labels and isolates outliers by random partitioning, which is cheap and explainable.

An autoencoder path exists (PyTorch, optional install) and is handled correctly by the metric layer, including its inverted sign convention. No autoencoder result is published: it did not earn a place over the Isolation Forest on this data.

Turning scores into an alert, without cheating

This is the part that took the work. Three problems have to be solved in order, and each one is a precondition for the next.

1. is_anomaly cannot be used at all

contamination=c guarantees that fraction of the training window is labelled anomalous, and the training window is the part assumed healthy. Measured: 5.03%, 5.08% and 8.01% of training files flagged, against configured 0.05, 0.05 and 0.08. So the label is doing what it was asked to do, and the first flagged file lands at or near file 0 on every run. The lead time is therefore read from a score threshold, never from is_anomaly.

2. A single flagged window is not an alert

Runs of consecutive flags occur by chance at those rates — a 10-of-10 rule still fires at file 9 of Tests 2 and 3. The rule is k of the last m files flagged.

3. Choosing k and m is where a result gets fabricated

Picking the (k, m) that yields the nicest lead time is precisely the failure the sustained rule exists to prevent, and it would be invisible in the output. Two things prevent it here:

The candidate list is fixed in advance, ordered by window length then strictness — 3-of-5, 4-of-5, 5-of-5, 6-of-10, … 30-of-30 — and the first entry that does not fire on healthy data wins. That is the most sensitive rule meeting the constraint, not the best-scoring one. Lead time is never consulted during selection.

The healthy region is split into three disjoint windows, so no window both sets a parameter and scores it:

Window Slice of the live series Job
Calibration first 10% sets the score threshold, at a 1% per-file false-alarm rate
Selection 10% – 25% chooses k and m
Held out 25% – 40% reports the false-alarm rate — never touched before this

The reported “0 false alarms in 323 / 147 / 949 files” comes from the third window only, which is what makes it out-of-sample.

The 40% boundary is itself an assumption — a run that degraded early would push the “healthy” window into the failure and report a real detection as a false positive — so it is checked rather than assumed: check_healthy_region verifies RMS stays below 2× baseline throughout. All three runs pass.

4. The post-shutdown tail

Each rig auto-terminates on a debris-triggered switch, and the acquisitions after that read near-zero RMS on every channel. They are not healthy readings, and they must not anchor a lead time. The tail is measured per run, not assumed — 0 files for Test 1, 2 for Test 2, 1 for Test 3 — by requiring every bearing to fall below 0.2× baseline, because one bearing can go quiet through damage but all four go quiet only when the shaft stops. Test 1 never went quiet at all and ends on its RMS peak, so the usual “drop the last file” would have discarded the very failure being measured. Details in the data documentation.

What this does not establish

  • The detector uses no frequency-domain feature. Everything it scores is a time-domain statistic or a rolling transform of one. The defect-frequency machinery is implemented and tested but not joined to the pipeline, so this result neither supports nor refutes the claim that bearing kinematics improve early detection. Connecting the two, and ablating the difference, is the next piece of work.
  • Three runs is three samples. The alert rule is selected per run against that run’s own healthy data. That is correct procedure, and it is not the same as validating a fixed rule on an unseen rig.
  • Zero false alarms is an upper bound. 147 to 949 held-out files cannot resolve a rate below roughly 1 in 1,000.
  • Lead time is anchored on the end of the run. The rigs ran to destruction, so the last live acquisition is taken as the failure — but no independent ground-truth failure timestamp exists to check that against, and it ties the number to when the rig happened to stop.
